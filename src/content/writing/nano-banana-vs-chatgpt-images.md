I pay for the two subscriptions that matter here: OpenAI's, which gives me
ChatGPT Images 2.0, and Google's, which gives me Nano Banana 2 inside the
Gemini app and its video tool. I've used both this year on real jobs: brand
stills, character sheets for short videos, posters with text on them, edits to
photos that already existed. Benchmarks in this category go stale in a week.
The split by job has held up longer.

## The two models

**ChatGPT Images 2.0** (April 2026) is the one that thinks before it draws.
Give it a layout problem, a lot of text, or a design brief with a point of
view, and it reasons through it like a decent art director. It can produce up
to eight images in one go and keep the same people and objects across them. It
took the top of the public image leaderboard the day it launched.

**Nano Banana 2** (February 2026) is the fast, grounded generalist. It goes up
to 4K, holds several people and a dozen-plus objects consistent across a set,
and it's wired into live web search, so "the actual wave wall on Fort
Lauderdale beach at sunset" comes back plausible instead of invented. Edits
are conversational and quick. There's a Lite version that returns an image in
about four seconds and a Pro version for the hard cases.

## The split I use

| The job | I reach for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Poster, menu, slide, infographic, anything with real text | ChatGPT | Text is print-clean and the layout has intent. Analytics Vidhya ran ten workplace jobs through both in April and ChatGPT took eight; text-heavy design was the widest gap, and that matches what I see. |
| Edit one thing in an existing photo, keep everything else | Nano Banana | Fast, conversational, and it drifts the rest of the frame less. Ten edits in the time ChatGPT takes to think about three. |
| A character I need to draw fifty times | Nano Banana for the reference sheet, then whichever model is holding the set | The sheet locks the face and wardrobe; after that, consistency is about your references, not the model. |
| A real place, a real object, a specific era | Nano Banana | Search grounding. It knows what the thing looks like instead of guessing. |
| Eight variations of one idea, fast | Nano Banana Lite | Four seconds each. Use volume to find the idea, then switch models to finish it. |
| The one hero image that has to be right | ChatGPT, thinking mode on | Slower by half a minute, better the first time. |
| A whole set that has to feel like one photographer shot it | One long ChatGPT thread | I keep a single conversation per project so the grade and the lens stay put across every image. |

Two things the table hides. First, on pure photorealism at a casual glance
they're close enough that I stopped having a preference. Second, on "cluttered
by default": Nano Banana's designs lean busier and more graphic out of the
box; ChatGPT's lean toward minimal and professional. Whether that matters
depends on whether you're making a flyer for a taco truck or a cover for a
report.

## What each costs

Through the apps, image generation is included in the subscription, with the
usual daily ceilings. Through the API, an image from OpenAI's model runs from
a bit over half a cent at the lowest quality to about twenty cents at the
highest, at standard size; edits with reference images bill higher. Google's
Lite model is the cheapest thing in either family, and its video model is
priced by the second, ten cents each. If you're making a few images a day, the
subscription you already have is the right answer and the API math is a
distraction.

## Where each one falls down

ChatGPT's thinking mode is a real fifteen-to-thirty seconds per image, which
doesn't sound like much until you're on your fortieth iteration. Its logo
reproduction is unreliable; check every mark by hand. And its instant mode,
the free one, is a noticeably different product from the paid thinking mode.

Nano Banana gets confused by long multi-step instructions in a single prompt.
In that same April test it repeated a poster three times instead of
translating it, and handed back a written script when asked for a comic. Break
the ask into turns and it behaves. It's a conversational editor, not a
one-shot art director.

Both stamp their output with provenance metadata now, and both will
occasionally refuse something innocent. Neither will reproduce a specific
living person on purpose.

## The thing neither can do

Neither model can tell you what the picture is for. That was the skill before
these tools and it's the skill after. Every image I've been proud of this year
started as three sentences about who would see it and what it had to make them
feel, and every image I threw away started as "make something cool for the
header."

Don't pick a model. Pick per job, the way you'd pick
a screwdriver, and don't collect tools you haven't needed twice. Then spend
the time you saved on the brief, because the brief is where the work still
lives.
