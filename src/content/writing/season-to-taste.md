Near the end of most recipes, after the exact grams, the oven temperature, and
the minutes, you get three words and the recipe walks away: season to taste.

That line is the recipe admitting something. It can get you close. It can't
finish the dish, because finishing takes someone standing over the pot with a
spoon, deciding.

I keep coming back to that line, because I think it describes where we are with
AI better than most of what gets written about AI.

## Everyone has the same groceries

Walk into a good grocery store and you can buy nearly everything a serious
restaurant cooks with. The olive oil, the flaky salt, the dry-aged steak, the
saffron. Nobody at the register asks for your credentials. If you can pay,
it's yours.

Building things got there too. The strongest AI models in the world are
available to anyone with a credit card. Open-source code for almost any common
problem is a search away. Data that used to take a research team is a
download. One person working alone has the same reasoning engines as a company
with ten thousand engineers.

So the inputs stopped being the advantage. And something less obvious happened
along the way: logic got cheap too. Ask a model to reason through a pricing
strategy, draft a contract clause, refactor a function, or outline an essay,
and it will do a competent job in seconds. Not always a great job. A competent
one.

Competent is the commodity now. When competent costs nothing, it's worth
nothing, because everyone has it. The default output of every model is roughly
the average of everything it learned from, which means the default is exactly
what everyone else is getting.

If everyone owns the same groceries, the groceries can't explain why one
dinner is unforgettable and the other one is fine.

## The recipe can't taste the soup

Give ten people the same recipe and the same ingredients and you'll get ten
different dishes. A few will be flat. One or two might be great. The recipe
didn't change. The cooks did.

A flat dish usually isn't wrong. Every step was followed and every measurement
was right. It's just missing something, and a person who has cooked a lot knows
what that something is before they can say it out loud. Often it's acid: a
squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar and the whole thing wakes up. Sometimes
it's salt. Sometimes it needs another ten minutes to cook down. Sometimes one
ingredient is shouting over the others and has to come out.

None of that is in the recipe, because the recipe can't know. This week's
tomatoes are sweeter than last week's. This stove runs hot. Samin Nosrat built
a whole book, *Salt, Fat, Acid, Heat*, on the idea that good cooks don't
memorize recipes. They understand a few elements well enough to adjust
anything.

A great chef isn't someone who follows instructions better than you. It's
someone who tastes constantly, notices what's off, fixes it, and tastes again.
The spoon never goes back in the drawer.

Now swap the kitchen for a workflow. You wire together a model, a prompt, some
data, an automation. It runs. Every step executes exactly as designed. The
output is correct, polished, and flat. It reads like everything else. It
answers the question without anyone seeming to care about the answer.

The system did what the recipe said. Nobody tasted it.

## What taste means when you're building systems

Taste sounds like a snob word, something you either have or you don't. I think
it's more practical than that. In a system, it shows up as a handful of
specific judgments.

**Knowing what good looks like before you see it.** You can't steer toward a
target you've never experienced. Chefs build a palate by eating at great
restaurants and by eating their own failures. Builders do it the same way:
spending time with the best products, the best writing, the work that made them
stop and pay attention, and being honest about how far their own work falls
short.

Ira Glass has a well-known riff on this. People get into creative work because
they have good taste, and for years what they make doesn't live up to it. That
gap hurts, and the only way through it is a lot of work. I find that
encouraging. The gap means the taste is doing its job. It's the thing telling
you the soup is flat.

**Knowing what to cut.** Most bad dishes have too much in them. Most bad
systems do too. AI made adding free: another feature, another step, another
agent, another paragraph. The model will happily build all of it. The judgment
is in taking things out until everything left is pulling its weight. Four
ingredients in balance beat fifteen that are fighting each other.

**Understanding the fundamentals instead of memorizing moves.** What stood out
to me studying how Miposhka drafts for Team Spirit at The International is that
the best Dota 2 captains don't win by copying the lineups that won last
tournament. They understand the matchups underneath: why this hero beats that
one, what a team wants to do and when it's weakest. That understanding lets
them see what will work in a situation nobody has played before. A recipe is a
draft someone else picked. Taste is understanding the matchups.

**Harmony over complexity.** The goal isn't the most impressive system. It's
the one where every part serves the result and nothing is showing off. Great
kitchens often send out plates that look simple, and the simplicity is the
hard part.

Taste isn't only about how things look, either. Deciding what a system should
refuse to do, when it should hand off to a person, and what it should never say
to a customer: that's taste too, and it's usually where the damage happens when
nobody is holding the spoon.

## The thumbprint is the product

If you build things, the temptation right now is to keep shopping. A better
model came out, so switch. A new framework, so rebuild. A new automation tool,
so rewire everything. That's buying fancier groceries, and it feels like
progress because it's work.

But the ingredients will keep getting better and cheaper, for you and for
everyone else at the same time. That's what makes them a commodity. Whatever
edge you get from the newest model lasts about as long as it takes everyone
else to click the same upgrade button.

What doesn't commoditize is the person holding the spoon. Your palate is built
from everything you've paid attention to, everything you've made and hated,
and every time you cut something you loved because the dish was better without
it. Nobody can download that. And it compounds: every piece of work you make
and judge honestly makes the next judgment a little sharper.

So spend more of your attention on great work than on new tools. Before you
ship anything, read it the way the person receiving it will, and ask whether
it's flat. When it is, figure out what's missing or what's shouting before you
reach for a bigger model. Cut one thing from everything you make. And make a
lot, because the gap between what you can recognize and what you can produce
only closes with reps.

The models will do more and more of the cooking. What's left is the part the
recipe always left to you.

Season to taste.
