A dollar is a piece of paper, or lately a row in a database, that everyone
agrees to treat as valuable because everyone else does. A corporation is a
legal ghost that can own things and be sued but that nobody has ever shaken
hands with. A market is what happens when enough people believe both of those
stories at once.

That's the frame I took from Harari and haven't been able to put down: the
things that let large numbers of strangers cooperate are shared fictions.
Money, nations, gods, companies. None of them exist the way a rock exists. All
of them are real in the only sense that matters for getting through a Tuesday,
which is that other people act as if they are.

Capitalism is the fiction where you get paid for serving strangers well. That
is the entire design. Adam Smith's line about the butcher and the brewer, that
we get our dinner not from their kindness but from their self-interest, gets
quoted as if it were cynical. Read the sentence again. It describes a system
where the way to get what you want is to make something someone else wants
first. Serve, then get served, and the price is what lets it scale past the
people you know.

What people forget is that Smith wrote a whole other book first, seventeen
years earlier, about sympathy, about the "impartial spectator" we carry around
who judges our conduct. He never thought the market ran on its own. He thought
it ran inside a society of people who could feel shame. Take the shame out and
you get a different machine.

## Two kinds of business

I've spent the last decade building small companies inside this fiction, and
part of my week documenting people who abuse it. That contrast has been the
best education in economics I've had, and it reduces to one distinction.

A **coordinating** business makes trust cheaper. You see the name, you know
what you'll get, you don't have to check. Every transaction it completes
leaves a little more trust in the world than it found. A good plumber. A
restaurant you'd send your mother to. A tool that does what the box says.
These businesses compound, because trust compounds: each satisfied stranger
lowers the cost of the next one.

An **extractive** business spends trust instead of building it. It uses the
signals of the coordinating kind, the professional website, the confident
voice, the testimonial, to get one transaction it doesn't deserve, and it
leaves the world a little less able to trust the next honest operator. A scam
is just a fiction with a payload. But it isn't only scams; plenty of legal
businesses live on confusion rents, the fee you didn't notice, the
subscription you forgot, the price that only exists because comparison is
hard, and they're extractive with better paperwork.

Same system, opposite bets. The system doesn't care which one you run. It pays
both, in the short run. That's the honest criticism of capitalism, and I think
it's right: markets price what can be priced, and the things a good life is
made of, time with people, trust, a sense of meaning, don't price well. So the
machine under-produces them and over-produces whatever's easiest to sell. Look
at your feed if you want the evidence.

## The receipts

I co-founded a marketplace in college that grew to about forty thousand users
and around four hundred thousand dollars a year. It was a coordinating
business: students needed a thing, other students had it, we made the finding
cheap. I sold it for less than it was worth after a governance failure I
should have seen coming, which taught me the other half of Smith's lesson. The
fiction only holds when the terms are clear and both sides can feel shame.
When that breaks, no contract saves you, and the market does not come to your
rescue. It wasn't built to.

Years later I started keeping a public directory of how specific scams
actually work, step by step, because I kept watching people I knew fall for
the extractive kind and I wanted a place to point them. I've never charged a
victim and I won't. Doing that work next to the building work is what made the
difference between the two kinds stop being theory. You can feel, in your hands, which kind
of thing you're making.

The question I'd put to any business, or job: does it leave
more trust in the world after each transaction, or less? Not your intentions.
The actual residue. The tell isn't the product. It's whether you'd be
comfortable if the customer could see your margin and exactly how you got it.
I run every offer I make through that question, and it has killed a few.

## The question career advice skips

Most career advice is about which fiction to join, and almost none of it is
about which kind of business to be inside it. People agonize over industry and
title and then spend twenty years running an extractive loop for someone else,
wondering why the money doesn't feel like anything.

The distinction gives you a filter that works at every scale. Freelancer,
founder, employee, it doesn't matter. Ask: is the value I'm paid for the
shadow of value I created, or is it the shadow of something the customer
didn't notice? The first kind of work compounds into a reputation and a life.
The second kind pays you to slowly become someone you'd have to hide from.

## The small kind

I'm for capitalism, the small kind, the kind Smith could have recognized:
people you could meet, selling things they'd stand behind, at terms both sides
understand. Build small. Own it. Compound. I'm skeptical of the large kind to
the exact degree that it's been separated from anyone who can feel shame, and
I think most of the reasonable critiques of the system are really critiques of
that separation.

But the personal version is simpler than the political one, and it's the part
you control. A business whose profit is the residue of the value it created is
the only kind that compounds, because it's the only kind trust will keep
coming back to. Serve first, get paid second, isn't a constraint on good unit
economics. Over a long enough window it *is* the unit economics.

So pick work where getting paid and doing good are the same act, not two acts
you balance. It exists in every industry, it's usually less glamorous than the
alternative, and it's the only kind of work I've ever seen make someone rich
in the way that counts, which is being glad of what you spent your years on.
