I know what a headache feels like. When someone else tells me their head hurts,
I believe them. I can see them wince or turn off the lights, but I can't feel
what they feel.

David Chalmers imagined someone who acts exactly like a person but has no
experience at all. He called it a philosophical zombie. It could complain
about a headache, ask for an aspirin and look relieved when the pain supposedly
went away. Nothing about how it behaved would give it away.

In 1950, Alan Turing called it a polite convention to assume that other people
think. It's a reasonable assumption: we have similar bodies and brains. A
chatbot can say it hurts without having either. The words alone don't tell
me whether anything hurts.

Still, I don't know that a brain is the only thing that could feel pain. If
there ever is a machine that can suffer, I'd rather have thought about what
could count as evidence than wait for it to ask nicely.

*David Chalmers, The Conscious Mind (1996); Alan Turing, Computing Machinery
and Intelligence (1950).*
