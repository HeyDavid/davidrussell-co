There's a genre now. The volunteer day with a photographer. The "giving back"
carousel. The caption that thanks the organization and tags it. I've seen
enough of it this year that I've started to think the golden rule has a quiet
half nobody talks about, and that the quiet half is the part that does the
work.

The public half is familiar: do for others what you'd want done for you. The
quiet half is older, and every tradition that wrote down the first part wrote
down the second: do it where nobody's watching. Don't let your left hand know
what your right hand is doing. Maimonides ranked eight levels of giving, and
the anonymous gift to an anonymous recipient sits near the top, above every
public one; the only thing higher is helping someone stop needing help. Marcus
Aurelius said a good person should do good the way a vine produces grapes,
without looking around afterward for someone to notice.

None of them were being modest for its own sake. They'd noticed something
mechanical about how service works.

## What posting does to it

Service is a relationship. Someone needed something, you were there, and now
there's a thread between you that didn't exist before. The act isn't the
value. The thread is.

The moment you post it, the act becomes content and the thread becomes a prop.
You didn't do it for them anymore; you did it for the third party watching,
and the person you helped can feel the difference, and so can everyone else.
The value doesn't just diminish. It evaporates, on contact with the audience,
because the audience was never supposed to be in the room.

I think most people know this and post anyway, because the incentives are loud
and the rule is quiet. Which is exactly why it needs to be a rule.

## The rule I've set

One standing commitment. Same place, same people, a few hours a month, for at
least six months before I'm allowed to add another. It goes on the calendar
like any fixed thing and it doesn't get renegotiated weekly.

And it never becomes content. Not a post, not a mention, not a line in a bio.
If it ever shows up publicly it'll be because someone else said it, and I'd
rather they didn't. I'm not going to tell you what it is. That's the point.

The rule came from a question I asked myself and didn't like the answer to:
what's the last good thing you did that no one will ever find out about, and
how long ago was it?

## The case for posting it

There's a real argument for visible generosity: it recruits. People who give
publicly get others to give, and a foundation that announces its pledge moves
more money than one that doesn't. Fair. If you're moving millions, announce
it. The rule is for the rest of us, the ones whose giving is measured in
hours, where the only thing an announcement moves is our own reputation.

## Two halves

A life you actually like has a public half and a quiet half. The public half
is the work, the writing, the thing you're building under your name; it's
supposed to be seen, that's how it serves anyone. The quiet half is the
service, and it's what keeps the public half honest. Without it, everything
you make becomes a bid for attention, and you can feel yourself becoming a
person who's always performing, even at dinner.

With it, you have a place where you're just useful, to someone who can't pay
you back and doesn't know your follower count. Be there for them. Then don't
tell anyone.

Don't ask me what mine is.
