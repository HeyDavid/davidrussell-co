"Agnostic" gets used as a soft word. It sounds like a shrug, the position of
someone who hasn't gotten around to deciding. That's not what the man who
coined it meant. Thomas Huxley made the word up in 1869 for a specific and
fairly aggressive claim: that you should not say you know a thing you cannot
demonstrate, and that this applies to the biggest question of all, in both
directions.

I've held that position for a long time. What I haven't done is argue it out
loud, and the only way I know to find out whether a position is mine or just a
habit is to argue the other side as hard as I can.

## The advocate for belief

Start with what's actually in front of you. Every civilization that ever
lasted built itself around something it treated as sacred. Every one. Not
because people were stupid but because a community needs a reason to keep
promises to people who can't enforce them, and "because I said so" has never
once been enough. The traditions that survived are the ones that produced
people who kept their word, buried their dead with care, took in strangers,
and forgave debts. Those aren't small outputs. You can call the source a
fiction, but the fruit is real, and you're eating it.

Now the harder point. You don't actually live as a skeptic. You trust that
your memories are yours, that other people have minds, that the sun will come
up, that your life means something. You have no proof of any of it. You've
verified exactly one consciousness in your life and it's the one reading this
sentence. Everything else you take on faith, and you're fine with that,
because you have to be in order to get out of bed. The believer just extends
that same faith one step further, to the thing that makes the whole picture
cohere. Why is that step forbidden and all the others free?

And the last point, which the skeptic never likes. The people I've known with
the deepest peace, the ones who could sit with a dying friend without
flinching, mostly believed something. Whatever you think of their reasons, the
result is the thing you claim to want.

## The advocate for unbelief

Everything the believer said about fruit is true, and none of it is evidence.
That a story produces good behavior tells you the story is useful. It tells
you nothing about whether the story is accurate. Plenty of useful things are
false; kids behave for Santa.

The move from "you trust your memories" to "so trust this too" is the oldest
trick in the argument, and it's sleight of hand. My confidence that the sun
will come up is backed by every sunrise anyone has ever recorded. My
confidence that other people have minds is backed by their being built like
me, out of the same stuff, acting the way I act. Those are inferences from
evidence. Extending "trust" from those to a specific being with intentions who
wants specific things from me is not one more step of the same kind. It's a
different kind of claim.

And notice which being. There have been thousands of gods, each with a
confident population that felt exactly the peace the believer describes. They
can't all be right. Most of them, by the believer's own reckoning, were wrong,
and felt no less certain for it. Certainty is a feeling, and feelings point in
ten thousand directions at once.

Bertrand Russell put the honest version well: to a philosopher he was an
agnostic, because you cannot disprove a god any more than you can disprove his
famous teapot orbiting the sun. To everyone else he was an atheist, because he
lived as though the Olympian gods weren't there, and so does everyone reading
this, about every god but one. The skeptic's ask is small. Be consistent.

## What neither advocate gets to claim

Each side's strongest
material is about *effects*, peace, community, consistency, honesty, and each
side's weakest material is the actual metaphysical claim. The believer can't
demonstrate the being. The skeptic can't demonstrate its absence, and has to
fall back on "the burden isn't on me," which is correct and also not an
answer.

What's left in the middle is Huxley's actual position, and it's not soft at
all. It's a discipline: hold your confidence at the level your evidence
supports, and no higher, in either direction. I keep a written ledger of
predictions with a percentage next to each one and I score it every quarter.
Agnosticism is the same practice pointed at the largest question. Not "I can't
decide." "Here is how much I'd bet, and here is why it's not more."

## The two questions people never separate

What's true, and how to live. Most people inherit an answer to the second from
whoever answered the first for them, and if the first answer ever cracks, the
second goes with it, and they're an adult with no reason to keep their
promises.

Agnosticism, done properly, forces the separation. It says: you don't get the
metaphysics, not yet, maybe not ever. Now what? And it turns out "now what"
has an answer that doesn't depend on the metaphysics at all.

Every tradition the believer pointed to, all of them, across five thousand
years and every continent, converges on one instruction. Confucius: don't
impose on others what you wouldn't want yourself. Hillel: what's hateful to
you, don't do to your fellow; the rest is commentary. The Mahabharata. The
Sermon on the Mount. Different gods, different cosmologies, one rule. Whatever
is going on at the top of the picture, the people down here kept arriving at
the same thing, independently, as the way to live together.

That convergence is the most useful fact in this entire debate, and it's a
fact about the instruction, not the being. The golden rule is the one claim
that survives every possible answer to the God question. If there's a god,
it's what every one of them asked for. If there isn't, it's still the thing
that makes a life work. It's the only bet on the board that pays out in every
world.

## Where I land

So: I don't know, and I mean it as a position, not a pause. I hold the
question open, I keep my number honest, and I refuse to let the openness at
the top leak into vagueness at the bottom, because the bottom was never in
doubt. Do the work well. Keep your word. Be useful to people who can't pay you
back. Sit with the ones who are hurting. You can commit to all of that with
your whole life and never settle the metaphysics, and I'd argue that
committing to it *without* the metaphysics is the more honest version, because
you're not doing it for a reward.

If you believe, I'm not trying to move you; the best people I know are
scattered across every answer. I'm trying to say that the thing you and I both
actually have to do tomorrow morning is identical, and that's worth more than
agreeing about the rest.
