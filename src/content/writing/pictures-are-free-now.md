In March of last year OpenAI turned on image generation inside ChatGPT and 130
million people made 700 million pictures in the first week, most of them of
themselves as characters in a Japanese animated film. The company's CEO said
the servers were melting. That was the moment pictures stopped being scarce,
and everything since has been the industry catching up to what that means.

Here's the eighteen months in one paragraph. Google shipped a model in August
2025 that people nicknamed Nano Banana, and 200 million edits happened in a
few weeks, mostly people turning selfies into little collectible figurines. A
Pro version followed in November. OpenAI pushed a faster model in December.
Google's second generation came in February: up to 4K, five people and a dozen
objects held consistent across a set, grounded in live web search. OpenAI
answered in April with ChatGPT Images 2.0, which took the top of the public
leaderboard on launch day and retired DALL-E, the model that started all this,
three weeks later. A "Lite" version from Google in June makes an image in
about four seconds. Midjourney's eighth version has been in testing since
spring. Google now says its app alone produces 150 million images a day.

I'm not going to review any of them here. I want to talk about what changed
underneath, because it's bigger than which model is ahead this month.

## The three things that used to fail

If you used these tools in 2023 you remember the tells. Text came out as
alphabet soup. Hands had six fingers or two. And you could not get the same
face twice, which meant you couldn't tell a story, make a brand, or build a
character, because every image was a stranger.

All three are solved for practical purposes. Text renders well enough to print
a menu. Hands are hands. Both major models will now hold a cast of characters
across a whole set of images, which was the actual wall between "fun toy" and
"production tool." I've made brand stills, character sheets, and whole visual
sets for short videos this year without touching a camera for the middle of
the process, and the failure rate on the things that used to fail is low
enough that I stopped counting.

And the price collapsed at the same time. The API cost of a decent image is
now a little over half a cent at the low end and about twenty cents at the
high end. Video from Google's newest model is ten cents a second. For most of
history the cost of a picture was a person's afternoon. It's now a rounding
error.

## What "prompting" turned into

There was a year where "prompt engineering" was a job title, and it made
sense: the models were bad at understanding you, so you learned their dialect.
That's mostly gone. The newer models reason about the request before they
draw, and the best way to talk to them is the way you'd brief a person: what
it's for, who's going to see it, what has to stay fixed, what to avoid. A good
brief for a designer is a good brief for the model.

Which is a quiet, important shift. The skill didn't disappear. It moved
upstream, from knowing the tool's quirks to knowing what you actually want.
That second skill was always the rare one. The tools just stopped hiding it.

## What's still scarce

If pictures are free, what are you being paid for, or what are you spending
your evenings on? Four things, in the order I've come to respect them.

**An idea underneath.** A generated image with nothing behind it is the visual
version of a paragraph that says nothing well. Audiences can tell in about a
second. The models can render any idea. Supplying one is still on you.

**Taste.** The default output of every model is the average of everything it's
seen, which means the default is exactly the look everyone else gets. Getting
something that could only be yours takes references, a point of view, and the
willingness to throw away twenty images to keep one. That ratio doesn't
improve much with better models, because "better" keeps redefining the
average.

**The rights.** Here's one I learned the expensive way this year, or nearly
did. A vendor's pricing page said "commercial use." Its terms of service said
free users may only use the product for non-commercial purposes. The terms are
the contract. The bullet point is marketing. I had a whole project's audio
generated under the wrong terms before I caught it. The fix is to pay for the
plan and regenerate every file: a few dollars and an afternoon. The lesson is
worth more than that: with generated media, read the terms, not the feature
list, and log the license at the moment you make the thing. The European rules
requiring machine-readable marks on generated content land in December, and
both big providers already stamp their output. Provenance is going from
optional to assumed.

## The one nobody talks about

Finishing. The tools are so good at starting that starting has become a way to
avoid finishing. This year I built the whole system before I made one thing
with it. That's a new failure mode, and the tools created it: when generating
is free, the discipline moves entirely to publishing.

Which means the permanent asset, the thing you're actually building while the
pictures are free, is craft under your own name. A recognizable eye. A shelf
of finished things. The evidence that you can take a brief from an idea to a
published piece, repeatedly, without a team.

## Make fewer, for someone

The temptation of infinite pictures is to make more pictures. I'd push the
other way. The tools have removed every excuse between you and the one thing
that would actually serve someone: the explainer your parents' business
needed, the poster for the neighborhood thing, the story you've been meaning
to tell your kids. The making is free now. The deciding to make it, for
someone, and finishing it, is the whole job.

The tools didn't change the job. They took away the excuse.
