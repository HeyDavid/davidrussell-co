Every idea that comes across my desk gets three questions. Does it compound?
Is it asymmetric and evergreen, meaning small downside, large upside, and
still worth something in five years? Is it highly profitable, or at least
could it be? Yes to all three and I'll do it. A no on any one and I cut it,
even if it's fun, even if it's mine. The filter has killed more of my own
ideas than anyone else's.

I built it for business. What I didn't expect is that it's the most useful
thing I own for deciding how to spend a life. The business version is the easy
one.

## What compounding actually means

Compounding is when the output of a thing becomes an input to the same thing.
Money earns money. But the money version is the least interesting case,
because money compounds at market rates and skills, reputations, and
friendships compound faster.

A skill compounds: every hour of practice makes the next hour more productive.
Writing compounds: every essay makes you a clearer thinker for the next one,
and the archive keeps working while you sleep. A reputation compounds: each
kept promise lowers the cost of the next person trusting you. A friendship
compounds: twenty years of shared context is something no new person can give
you at any price. Health compounds, in both directions, which is the scary
part.

And the opposite. Some things don't compound; they just cost. An hour of feed
is gone and made the next hour slightly worse. A job that pays hourly and
teaches nothing is a job you'll have to do again tomorrow at the same rate. A
relationship you maintain out of obligation is rent, and rent builds nothing.

The filter's first question, applied to a life, is just: which of my hours are
earning something, and which are only being paid out?

## Asymmetric and evergreen

The second question has two halves. Asymmetric means the downside is capped
and the upside isn't. Evergreen means it'll still be true, or still be useful,
in five years, ten, fifty.

Most of what looks urgent fails both halves. The trend, the platform, the tool
of the month, the argument everyone's having this week: high effort, symmetric
risk, gone by spring. And most of what actually matters passes both without
trying. Sleep. Reading things that were true a century ago and will be true in
another one. Learning to write a clear paragraph. Being the person who shows
up. The downside of any of these is a few hours. The upside is the shape of
your life.

I keep a written list of predictions about what will change over the next few
years, with a confidence next to each, and I score it every quarter. But
underneath that list there's a second one, of things I'm betting won't change
at all: people will still gather in rooms, still sing, still run, still
grieve, still sit with the dying. The scarce things will still be the human
ones. Nothing on that list is scoreable, and that's the point. The first list
tells me where the money is this decade. The second list tells me how to live,
and it's the more valuable of the two by a wide margin.

The rule I've landed on: **own the constants, rent the changes.** Build your
life on the things that won't move. Use the things that will, cheaply, and be
ready to drop them.

## The melt-water law

Every advantage melts. The new tool everyone hasn't found yet, the platform
before it fills up, the skill before it's common: each one is a temporary gap
between what something costs you and what it's worth to someone else, and
every gap closes. I've watched several close in my own career.

The people who come out ahead aren't the ones who found the gap first. They're
the ones who used the temporary spread to buy something permanent while it was
open. A relationship. A track record. A body of work. A skill that transfers.
The ones who didn't just made some money for a while and then had to find the
next gap, and the next, forever.

That's the third question in disguise. "Highly profitable" was never about the
margin on one deal. It's about whether the deal leaves you holding something
that keeps paying after the deal is over.

## The failure mode

There's a version of this that turns you into a miser of your own time, a
person who can't enjoy an afternoon unless it's building equity, and that's a
worse life than the one it's optimizing. Some of the best things don't
compound. A day at the beach is just a day at the beach. I try to hold the
filter for decisions, not for moments: it decides what I build and commit to,
and it stays out of the room once I'm there.

And compounding is slow at the start, invisibly slow, and most people quit in
the flat part of the curve. The filter can tell you what to do. It can't make
the first two years feel like anything. Only the practice does that.

## Run it on your life

Run the three questions on your life the way you'd run them on a business, and
notice what survives. It's almost always the same short list. The people you'd
call at three in the morning. The body you'll live in for the next fifty
years. The craft you're getting better at. The habit of telling the truth.
Those compound, they're asymmetric, they're evergreen, and they're profitable
in the only currency that doesn't melt.

Nearly everything on that list, you'll notice, is something you do with or for
someone else.
