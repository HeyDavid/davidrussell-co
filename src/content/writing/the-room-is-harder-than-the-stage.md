I run in South Florida, which for half the year means running in a wet oven.
Some days it's scorching, there's trash smell coming off the canal, and
somebody's burning something upwind so there's smoke in my face for a mile.
Those are the days I go anyway. Then I noticed something that eventually
turned into the way I run everything: on the rare morning that's cool and
clear, running feels easy. Not easier, easy. Like it isn't the same activity.

Nothing about my legs changed. The room changed. I'd been practicing in a
harder room than the one I was performing in, and the performance got the
difference for free.

## Where this came from

For years I played ranked everything. Dota, Starcraft, arena, whatever had a
ladder. Top ten percent, chasing top one. I quit because the math stopped
working: wins bought me focus and losses bought me a bad next morning, and the
losses were winning. But I learned one thing on those ladders that I haven't
found anywhere else, and it's this. The players who got good weren't the ones
who played the most. They were the ones who practiced against people better
than them, on purpose, and lost a lot, on purpose, and treated the losing as
the point.

Everyone else was doing the opposite. Queueing into games they'd win,
protecting the rating, feeling good. And staying exactly where they were.

It took me years to see it wasn't about games. The
room you practice in sets the ceiling on the room you can perform in. If
practice is comfortable, the stage will always be harder than anything you've
done, and you'll meet every hard moment for the first time in front of people.

## The law

So the one rule I run on now, in work and in everything I'm trying to get good
at: **the room is always harder than the stage.**

Make the practice deliberately unfriendly. No prep. Cold start. One take, no
re-recording the part where you flubbed the name. A hard timer that ends the
session whether or not you're done. Publishing the thing you're not sure
about. Being wrong out loud, on the record, where people can see it. Every one
of those is a rep, and every rep is training for something that will happen to
you later, when it counts: the client who asks the question you didn't prepare
for, the talk where the slide doesn't advance, the day you have to say "I
don't know, let me find out" and have it land as credibility instead of
weakness.

You cannot practice those on the stage. The stage is where you find out
whether you practiced.

## Lowering the pressure is how you raise the bar

This is the part people get backwards, and I got it backwards for years. You'd
think a harder room means more pressure. It's the opposite. The hardest room
I've ever been in is the one where the outcome doesn't matter, because that's
the only room where I'll actually attempt the thing I can't do yet.

My best work has always happened in the practice room, with the pressure off,
just getting in the zone and enjoying the practice of trying hard at something
hard, and showing up. When I show up and go for it regardless of whether it's
going to be perfect, there's no risk, and the strange result is that those are
my best performances. Then high performance stops being an event. It becomes
the norm, even on the off days, because pushing through is just what I do now.
It's natural. Nobody's watching, so I do it fully.

The rep is the deliverable. Whatever comes out of it, the video, the essay,
the sale, is a byproduct. Success is: I sat down and did the hard thing. That
scorecard has one line and I can't lose on it by being bad. I can only lose by
not showing up.

## The rules I keep

A few operating rules fell out of this. They sound severe. They're the
opposite; every one exists to take a decision off the table so that the only
decision left is "show up."

Nothing is re-recorded. One take is the format. If it breaks, that's the
episode, and the break is usually the best part. The session ends at the
timer, even if it's twenty-two minutes in and the thing turned out to be
boring; a short honest session beats a padded one. Missing a week isn't a
failure. Two in a row is a signal to look at what's in the way, once, plainly,
and then drop it. I don't look at metrics for the first ninety days of
anything, because early numbers are noise and checking them is the fastest way
to put the pressure back on. I see one number: the streak.

And no gear until the streak is real. Equipment is the most common way a
practice habit turns into a shopping habit. If the streak breaks, nothing gets
bought until it's rebuilt.

## Readiness is a memory

Most people are waiting to feel ready, and readiness doesn't come from
waiting. It comes from having already been somewhere harder. The confidence
you want on the stage isn't a feeling you can summon; it's a memory of the
room. If you don't have the memory, no amount of preparation substitutes for
it, and you'll spend your life over-preparing for things that only reps could
have prepared you for.

And the room is where the fulfillment is. I used to think the good feeling lived on the stage, in
the win, the applause, the number going up. It doesn't. The stage is a
readout. The good feeling lives in the rep, in the hour where the pressure is
off and you're fully trying at something hard, and every time I've chased it
on the stage instead I've come back empty.

## Mostly practice rooms

A life you actually like is mostly practice rooms. Pick the few things worth
getting good at, build a room for each that's harder than where you'll
perform, and show up to it on a fixed day whether or not you're any good that
day. Enjoy the trying, and ship the rep.

Then notice what the reps are for. Every hard room I've built has made me more
useful to someone else: steadier on the call, clearer on the stage, calmer for
the friend who needed someone calm. The practice is for you. The performance
was always for them.
