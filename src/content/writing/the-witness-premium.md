Google says its Gemini app now turns out more than 150 million images a day.
Video from the same company costs ten cents a second. Trust in news hit 37
percent worldwide in this year's Reuters Institute report, the lowest since
they started counting, and 25 percent in the United States. Social and video
feeds passed television as the main place people get news. YouTube alone is
13.8 percent of all the time Americans spend in front of a TV, more than any
network or streamer, and it isn't a network or a streamer. It's mostly people
in rooms, talking.

And one number going the other way. In 2023, a large influencer agency asked
consumers whether they preferred AI-generated creator content to the regular
kind, and 60 percent said yes. This January the same question got 26 percent.
Meanwhile the app OpenAI built to be a feed of nothing but generated video
shut down in March.

I run AI systems for a living. I use these models every day. None of this is a
complaint about the machines. It's what happens to the price of a thing when
its supply goes infinite.

## What happens when supply goes infinite

When something becomes free to make, its price goes to zero, and it goes fast:
months, not a decade. What's valuable afterward is whatever the flood didn't
touch.

Recorded music went through this. When a song became a file, the song stopped
being the product. What people kept paying for, and pay more for every year,
is the show: a specific night, in a specific room, with the person who made it
standing there. Concert prices set records while streams pay fractions of a
cent, and nobody finds that strange anymore.

Media in 2026 is the same story at a bigger scale. Text, image, voice, and now
video are effectively free to produce. Which means "content" has stopped being
scarce, and the value has moved to what's still scarce around it. I count four
things: attention, trust, meaning, and a witness.

The last one is the one I keep coming back to.

## What a witness is

A witness is someone who was there. Someone who put their name on what they
saw, will still be around next year to be wrong about it in public, and has
something to lose if they are.

You can't generate that. You can generate a face and a voice and a confident
paragraph, and increasingly you can't tell it from the real thing on
inspection. But the value of a witness never
came from inspection. It came from the track record and the stake. It's the
difference between a recipe and your grandmother telling you the recipe is
wrong.

Look at who's actually winning on the platforms right now. Not the accounts
posting forty synthetic clips a day; the platforms now label that and mostly
won't pay for it. The winners are one person, on camera, in their own voice,
doing a real thing and being visibly uncertain about it. One talent agency
told Digiday in January that brands have started asking for the unmade bed in
the background on purpose. That's a price signal, not a style.

## Both sides of the screen

Two reasons, depending on which side of the screen you're on.

If you're mostly consuming: the feeds are built to spend your attention for
you, and the material they're spending it on is now infinite and mostly
weightless. The old heuristic, "if it's in front of me it must matter," is
dead. The replacement is older and simpler: follow witnesses. People with
names, stakes, and a history you can audit. It's a shorter list than your
follow count, and it should be.

If you're making anything: the bar for making dropped to the floor and the bar
for mattering went up. You cannot out-volume a machine that produces 150
million images before lunch, and you shouldn't try, because volume is exactly
the thing that got repriced to zero. What you can be is the person in the
room. The one who tested it, who was wrong last time and said so, who shows up
next month.

That is slower. A witness only accumulates value over time, and only under a
real name. But it's the only position in media that compounds instead of
depreciating, and it's available to anyone willing to be seen trying.

## Where I might be wrong

Maybe the authenticity premium is a phase. Maybe the 26 percent climbs back as
the generated stuff gets better and people stop being able to tell or stop
caring. It's possible. I'd point at the concert data, which has been running
in one direction for twenty years through every improvement in recorded sound,
and say the burden's on the other side. But I'm watching the number.

## What this has to do with living well

The best media in your life was never media. It was the conversation that went
past midnight, the show you saw with someone, the thing a friend told you
they'd never told anyone. Those were valuable for the same reason a witness is
valuable: a person was there, and it cost them something.

Spend your attention on people
who were there. Make things the way a witness would, under your own name,
about what you actually saw, for the ten people who'll be glad you did.
Publish what you'd want to receive.

The machines are going to keep getting better at everything that can be
generated. Fine. Being there was never on that list.
