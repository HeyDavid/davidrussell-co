We say "pay attention," and the verb is exactly right. Attention is a payment.
It's the one resource you own that has no income side: you can't earn more
hours of it, you can't save it for later, and every unit you don't spend on
purpose gets spent anyway, by whatever's loudest.

William James wrote in 1890 that his experience was what he agreed to attend
to. Not what happened to him. What he attended to. That was a philosophical
point when he made it. It's an economic one now, because in the century since,
we built an industry whose entire product is deciding what you attend to, and
it's very good at its job.

## The spending machine

A feed is a slot machine with the payout replaced by content. Pull, and
sometimes something good comes up, and sometimes nothing, and the
unpredictability is the point; it's the same reward schedule that keeps a
pigeon pecking a button for hours. The people who built it know this.

And this year the supply on the other end went infinite; I've written about
the numbers elsewhere, and they're absurd. The machine no longer has any
constraint on the input side; the only scarce thing left in the whole system
is the part that's yours. Which means the machine is now competing for exactly
one resource, and it's the resource your life is made of.

I don't say that to be dramatic. I say it because it changes the math. When
content was scarce, being fed was a service. Now that content is infinite,
being fed is a cost, and the only question is whether you're the one paying or
the one being spent.

## What paying looks like

I know the difference from the inside, from years of chasing the locked-in
state where the world reduces to the next decision and three hours go by as
one. That state has conditions, and they turn out to be the conditions for
paying attention to anything.

A goal you actually care about, not a task. Feedback that's fast and honest.
Difficulty a little above what you can do. And distractions removed before you
start, not managed during, because I have never once gotten locked back in
after checking my phone. Not once, in years of trying. The phone goes in
another room before the session, or the session doesn't happen.

My cheapest working version: two hours, one tab, one document, phone out of
the building. The first twenty minutes feel like nothing is happening. The
real work lives in the back ninety. If you've never done this, the
uncomfortable part is discovering how loud the withdrawal is, how many times
in the first half hour your hand reaches for a thing that isn't there. That
reaching is the machine, still running.

## Upstream of everything

Attention is upstream of everything else you might care about. What you attend
to is what you learn, what you get good at, who you become. A person is
roughly the sum of what they've paid attention to for twenty years, and most
people never chose the list. It was chosen for them by whichever feed got
there first. That's not a small thing to hand over.

And because there's a version of this that isn't about productivity at all,
and it's the version I actually care about. Simone Weil said attention is the
rarest and purest form of generosity. Think about the last time someone gave
you theirs, fully, no phone, no half-listening, just there. It's rare enough
now that you can probably remember the specific conversation. It's the most
direct way one person can serve another, and it costs nothing except the one
thing that's scarce.

## Who to pay it to

Spend attention on things that give it back: work that leaves you more able,
books that were true before you were born, the two-hour block where you
finally do the hard thing. That's paying it. And then spend it on people, on
purpose, the way you'd spend money on someone you love, with the same
awareness of what it's worth.

Give the attention you'd want to receive. Whole, undivided, for the length of
a conversation. In a year when every other input to a life has become infinite
and free, it's the one gift that still costs the giver something, which is
exactly why it still means anything.
